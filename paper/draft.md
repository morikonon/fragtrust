## 1 Introduction

When files are deleted or a volume is quick-formatted, the file-system metadata that maps clusters to files is lost or unreliable, while much of the content stays on the medium. File carving recovers files from this content alone, typically by matching header and footer signatures and validating the candidate files [1]. Fragmentation breaks the contiguity that simple carvers assume [1], and a cluster from the middle of a file carries no signature at all. Fragment classifiers address this gap. They predict the file type of an isolated block of bytes, which a carver can use to select and order candidate blocks [2].

Since FiFTy introduced the FFT-75 benchmark [3, 4], reported accuracy on its 75-type scenario with 4096-byte blocks has risen from 77.5 % [3] to about 82–84 % [5–7], while a CNN with about 105k parameters already reaches 79.27 % [8]. The remaining errors concentrate on archives, compressed media and container formats whose blocks look alike [3]. A classifier for this task will therefore be wrong on a large share of blocks for some time. For an examiner, the useful question is whether the classifier knows when it is wrong.

Digital forensics lacks testing standards to validate its tools [9], and the opacity of machine-learning methods makes validation harder still [10]. A benchmark accuracy averaged over 75 types does not tell an examiner which labels in a given case can be relied on. Current systems use classifier scores heuristically. A recent carving architecture orders candidate blocks by a learned score and states that the score is "not a calibrated probability" [2]. Content-type detectors reject low-confidence outputs with thresholds tuned on validation data [11, 12], without a finite-sample guarantee. To our knowledge, whether the confidence of file fragment classifiers is calibrated has not been measured.

We study this question on the established FFT-75 task. We do not propose a new architecture and make no claim of state-of-the-art accuracy. Instead, we train gradient boosting on byte statistics, byte-level CNNs of three sizes and a small byte transformer under one protocol, and ask four research questions:

- **RQ1** How accurate are these model families, and how fast do they run on a CPU?
- **RQ2** Are their confidence scores calibrated, and which operating points with finite-sample guarantees do they support?
- **RQ3** How do the models and these guarantees behave on high-entropy blocks and on AES-CTR ciphertext and random data never seen in training?
- **RQ4** Do calibration and the guarantees carry over from benchmark blocks to the clusters of a controlled, quick-formatted FAT32 image, and what does recalibration on a small labelled part of the image recover?

Our contributions are:

1. To our knowledge, the first calibration analysis of file fragment classifiers on FFT-75 (ECE, NLL, Brier score, reliability diagrams), before and after temperature scaling, across a gradient-boosted baseline, byte CNNs and a byte transformer (Sect. 6.2).
2. Distribution-free guarantees for examiners: selective labelling with a Learn-then-Test bound on the accuracy of labelled blocks, and conformal prediction sets with marginal coverage. Prior fragment and content-type classifiers abstain or output "unknown" with heuristically tuned thresholds and no finite-sample guarantee [7, 11, 12]. We show that thresholds without a guarantee miss the target and that marginal coverage hides large per-type shortfalls (Sect. 6.2).
3. A measurement of how abstention and conformal sets behave on AES-CTR ciphertext and random blocks. Prior work showed that such blocks can receive confident labels [12]; we quantify whether the guarantees flag them and compare the model with a model-free byte-entropy baseline (Sect. 6.3).
4. A case study on a controlled, quick-formatted FAT32 image with ground truth. It tests whether calibration and both guarantees survive the shift from benchmark blocks to disk clusters, compares them with a header-signature carver, and measures what recalibration on 20 % of the image's files recovers. Earlier disk-image tests report accuracy only [3, 12] (Sect. 6.4).
5. A count of exact duplicates between the official FFT-75 test and training splits, and their effect on accuracy, which is small (Sects. 3 and 6.5).

Code, configurations and the image builder will be released upon acceptance.

## 2 Related Work

**File fragment classification.** Early classifiers used hand-crafted byte statistics with support vector machines, such as Sceadan [CITE: Beebe et al., Sceadan, IEEE TIFS 2013; full text not yet read, cite only after reading]. FiFTy [3] introduced FFT-75 (75 types; 512- and 4096-byte blocks) and a compact 1D CNN with 77.5 % accuracy on 4096-byte blocks, against 69.0 % for its re-run of Sceadan. Later work changed the architecture: depthwise-separable CNNs with about 105k parameters [8], byte-to-image ResNets [5], a dual-branch byte and image network [6], a recurrent-convolutional hybrid [7] and Swin transformers on raw bytes [13]. These works report top-1 accuracy on FFT-75's split, which shuffles blocks rather than files [3]. Tesserae and MoDiCo [14] move to a far larger corpus (619 types) with file-disjoint, deduplicated splits and report top-1, top-5 and balanced accuracy. To our knowledge, none of these works reports whether the predicted probabilities are calibrated. Tests on disk images exist but measure accuracy only: FiFTy dropped to 41.4 % on a DFRWS-2006 image with 512-byte clusters [3], and MimeLens classifies mid-file blocks of a constructed 1 GB ext4 image [12].

**Confidence and rejection in content-type detection.** Magika [11] classifies whole files. It tunes one confidence threshold per type on validation data at 99 % precision and falls back to a generic label when no type is confident. MimeLens [12] recommends labelling a block only above a per-class confidence threshold, and shows that ciphertext blocks collapse onto an "opaque" label with high confidence. ByteRCNN [7] adds an open-set evaluation. A 2026 carving architecture runs a learned block classifier but uses its score only to order candidates [2]. None of these thresholds carries a finite-sample guarantee on the accuracy of the accepted labels.

**High-entropy data.** Entropy tests cannot reliably separate encrypted from compressed fragments [15], and recent work trains dedicated classifiers for this distinction, with temperature scaling inside an ensemble [16]. We do not train on encrypted data. We ask whether a general classifier's abstention and prediction sets flag ciphertext it has never seen.

**Calibration, selective prediction and conformal sets.** Modern neural networks are often miscalibrated, and temperature scaling corrects much of this on in-distribution data [17]. ECE depends on its binning, so equal-mass bins are recommended next to equal-width ones [18]. Under dataset shift, post-hoc calibration degrades, while ensembles hold up better [19, 20]. Selective classification adds a reject option with a bound on the risk of the accepted predictions [21]. Learn-then-Test (LTT) casts the choice of such a threshold as multiple hypothesis testing with finite-sample error control [22]. Split conformal prediction turns any classifier into prediction sets with distribution-free marginal coverage [23]; LAC [24], APS [25] and RAPS [26] trade set size against adaptivity. With many classes, marginal coverage can hide per-class under-coverage [27]. All these guarantees assume that calibration and test data are exchangeable, which a change of medium can break [28]. The maximum softmax probability (MSP) and the energy score are standard baselines for detecting out-of-distribution inputs [29, 30]. To our knowledge, none of these tools has been applied to file fragment classification.

## 4 Methods

### 4.1 Models

**Gradient boosting on byte statistics.** Each block is described by 262 features: the normalised 256-bin byte histogram, its Shannon entropy, the mean and standard deviation of the byte values, the longest run of one byte value, the bigram entropy and the share of printable ASCII bytes. LightGBM [31] is tuned on a grid of 63 or 127 leaves and learning rates of 0.05 or 0.1 on 5,000 blocks per type, selected by val_a log-loss ({{results/tuning/lgbm/best.json:params/num_leaves|d}} leaves, learning rate {{results/tuning/lgbm/best.json:params/learning_rate|.2f}}), and then trained on the full subset with early stopping on val_a log-loss. Five seeds differ through feature and row bagging (fraction 0.8). Its log-probabilities serve as logits in all later steps.

**Byte CNNs.** A byte embedding feeds four blocks of 1D convolution, batch normalisation, GELU and max-pooling by a factor of four; mean and max pooling over positions feed a linear layer. CNN-M uses a 32-dimensional embedding, 64, 128, 128 and 256 channels and kernel size 9 ({{results/runs/cnn_m_s0/meta.json:params|,d}} parameters). CNN-S (16-dimensional embedding, 32 to 96 channels, kernel size 7) and CNN-L (64-dimensional embedding, 128 to 512 channels) test whether capacity limits accuracy [PENDING: parameter counts of CNN-S and CNN-L from their meta.json].

**Byte transformer.** Bytes are embedded in 32 dimensions and grouped into non-overlapping 16-byte patches, which are projected to 256 tokens of width 128. A class token and learned position embeddings are added, and four pre-norm encoder layers with four attention heads feed a linear head on the class token [PENDING: parameter count from transformer_s0/meta.json].

**Training.** Networks use AdamW with warm-up and cosine decay, batch size 512 and bfloat16 autocast; logits are saved in 32-bit precision. Training stops after five epochs without improvement in val_a accuracy (at most 30 epochs). We use no label smoothing, mixup or other regularisers that change confidence, because confidence is what we measure. CNN-M and the transformer are trained with three seeds, CNN-S and CNN-L with one. A deep ensemble [20] averages the probabilities of the three CNN-M seeds without further training.

### 4.2 Calibration

For logits z and temperature T, the predicted distribution is p = softmax(z/T), and the confidence of a block is its maximum probability. T is fitted on val_a by minimising the negative log-likelihood (NLL) [17]. We report on the test split, before (T = 1) and after scaling: the expected calibration error ECE = Σ_b (n_b/n)·|acc_b − conf_b| over 15 equal-width confidence bins; the same quantity over 15 equal-mass bins (adaptive ECE) [18]; the NLL; and the multiclass Brier score, the mean over blocks of Σ_k (p_k − 1[y = k])².

### 4.3 Selective Labelling with a Guarantee

A block is labelled automatically if its confidence s(x) reaches a threshold τ, and passed to the examiner otherwise. Coverage is the share of labelled blocks; selective accuracy is the accuracy among them. The score is the MSP after temperature scaling, with the negative predictive entropy as an alternative. We report the risk–coverage curve and the area under it (AURC) [21].

For a target accuracy 1 − ε of 95 % or 99 %, τ is fitted on val_b in two ways. The *empirical* threshold is the lowest one whose selective accuracy on val_b reaches the target, without a guarantee. The *LTT* threshold follows Learn-then-Test with fixed-sequence testing [22]: for accepted-set sizes m on a fixed grid of 100 sizes, in increasing order, we test the null hypothesis that the error rate among the m most confident blocks exceeds ε, with the exact binomial p-value P(Bin(m, ε) ≤ e_m), where e_m is the observed number of errors. The scan stops at the first p-value above δ = 0.05, and τ is the confidence of the m-th block for the largest size whose null hypothesis was rejected. Under exchangeability of val_b and test blocks, the selective error of the chosen threshold is then at most ε with probability at least 1 − δ over the draw of val_b. A set smaller than m_min = ⌈log δ / log(1 − ε)⌉ cannot pass even without errors ({{results/runs/cnn_m_s0/analysis/trust.json:selective/msp/targets/0.95/ltt/m_min|d}} blocks at 95 %, {{results/runs/cnn_m_s0/analysis/trust.json:selective/msp/targets/0.99/ltt/m_min|d}} at 99 %). Such sizes are skipped, which keeps the order of the tests fixed in advance; if no size passes, the model abstains on everything. A target counts as met only if at least one test block is labelled and the labelled blocks reach the target, since abstaining on everything makes no errors but is useless.

### 4.4 Conformal Prediction Sets

Split conformal prediction [23] computes a non-conformity score for every val_b block and its true type, takes the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile q̂ of these scores, and returns for a test block all types whose score is at most q̂. Under exchangeability, the set contains the true type with probability at least 1 − α, marginally over blocks. The scores are LAC, one minus the probability of the type [24]; randomised APS, the probability mass of the types ranked above it plus a uniformly random share of its own [25]; and RAPS, which adds a rank penalty λ·max(0, r − k_reg) to APS, with λ = 0.01 and k_reg = 2 fixed in advance [26]. For α ∈ {0.10, 0.05} we report coverage, set size and, because the guarantee is not class-conditional [27], coverage per type. APS at 95 % is the main setting later on.

### 4.5 High-Entropy and Unseen Data

We report accuracy, coverage at the 99 % LTT threshold and APS set size per bin of byte entropy (Shannon entropy of the byte histogram, in bits per byte). Bins are finer near 8 bits, because a uniformly random 4096-byte block has an expected empirical entropy of about 8 − 255/(2 · 4096 · ln 2) ≈ 7.955 bits. For the probes we report the AUROC for separating them from test blocks with the MSP, the predictive entropy and the energy score [29, 30] (networks only; energy is undefined for LightGBM's log-probabilities), the share abstained, the APS set size and the most frequent predicted types. As a model-free baseline, we also report the AUROC of the block's byte entropy itself: a model score adds information only where it beats this baseline.

### 4.6 Case Study: A Controlled FAT32 Image

**Image.** We build a 1 GB FAT32 image with 4096-byte clusters, so that one cluster is one block, using dosfstools and mtools without mounting. Zero-filled filler files of 32–512 KB fill the volume except a 64 MB tail. Every second filler in allocation order is then deleted, and the evidence files are copied in a fixed random order, so that files larger than a hole are fragmented. Finally, the image is quick-formatted with mkfs.fat. Ground truth comes from the FAT cluster chains before the format and is verified by reassembling every file against its SHA-256 hash; the build also checks that the format changed no evidence cluster. The evidence set consists of [PENDING: number of files, types, total size and fragmented files, from build.json; files collected by the authors from public sources, no personal data]. Labels follow the file extension mapped to the FFT-75 type, with manual overrides. Types outside FFT-75, such as encrypted containers, and free space are not scored for accuracy; for them we report confidence, the share abstained and the set size.

**Evaluation.** Every cluster of the formatted image is classified. On clusters of known type we report the metrics of Sects. 4.2–4.4 overall and by group: fragmented versus contiguous files, and the first (header), middle and last (tail) clusters of a file. The temperature, thresholds and conformal quantiles fitted on FFT-75 are transferred unchanged. To measure what local data recovers, we also refit them on 20 % of the image's labelled files, grouped by file so that the clusters of one file never fall on both sides, and evaluate both the transferred and the refitted parameters on the remaining 80 %. Over 20 random splits we report mean and standard deviation, the share of splits that meet the 99 % target and, separately, the share in which the refitted threshold labels nothing.

**Carver baseline.** A header-signature carver matches the start of each cluster against a signature table, specific signatures first; container formats (ZIP, OLE2, TIFF, ISO-BMFF) yield a set of candidate types. The *header-only* variant labels only matching clusters. The *propagated* variant extends a header's type to the following clusters until the next header or an all-zero cluster, the contiguity assumption that fragmentation breaks. We report coverage, accuracy of the answers (true type in the candidate set) and candidate-set size.

## 7 Discussion and Limitations

**Guidance for examiners.** The following conclusions rest on CNN-M and FFT-75 [PENDING: revise after the other models and the case study]. First, a confidence threshold read off a validation curve is not enough when a report states an accuracy. It missed the target on test in our runs, while LTT thresholds met it at a small cost in coverage. Second, a conformal set is a useful output for blocks the classifier does not label, but its coverage holds on average over types, not for each type. A statement such as "the true type is in this set with 95 % probability" does not hold for pdf or zip blocks in particular. Third, abstention on high-entropy blocks means "compressed or encrypted, type uncertain", not "encrypted". Byte entropy alone separates ciphertext almost as well, and the model's sets for ciphertext resemble those for compressed blocks. Fourth, [PENDING: whether parameters fitted on a benchmark can be used on a new medium, or must be refitted on known files from that medium (Sect. 6.4)].

**Limitations.** The case study uses one controlled image: one file system (FAT32), one cluster size equal to the block size, and a file collection chosen by us. Other file systems, cluster sizes and media, for example SSDs with TRIM, were not tested. Clusters of one file are correlated, so calibration and image data are not exchangeable. The recalibration splits by file to limit this, but the guarantees on the image are empirical, not formal. The LTT bound holds with probability 1 − δ over the calibration draw, so a single test pass that meets the target is consistent with the bound but does not prove it. Conformal coverage is marginal; we did not try class-conditional methods [27]. All models were trained on {{results/data/fft75_4096_s1_v1/manifest.json:subset/train_per_class|,d}} blocks per type rather than the full official training split, and CNN-S and CNN-L have one seed each. The leakage check finds exact duplicates only. The probes are synthetic, labels on the image follow file extensions, and we did not consider anti-forensic manipulation. Finally, we used FFT-75 rather than the larger Tesserae corpus [14] so that our results stay comparable with the accuracy figures of prior work; calibrating MoDiCo is left for future work.

## 8 Conclusion

We asked whether an examiner can trust the confidence of a file fragment classifier. On FFT-75, a byte CNN is already close to calibrated, and its confidence supports guarantees an examiner can use. LTT thresholds label {{results/runs/cnn_m_s0/analysis/trust.json:selective/msp/targets/0.99/ltt/coverage|pct1}} % of blocks at {{results/runs/cnn_m_s0/analysis/trust.json:selective/msp/targets/0.99/ltt/selective_accuracy|pct2}} % accuracy, and conformal sets reach their marginal coverage, though not for every type. The same thresholds abstain on ciphertext and random data, but a byte-entropy threshold separates these almost as well, so abstention should not be read as detecting encryption. [PENDING: one sentence answering RQ1 (model families and CPU cost) and one answering RQ4 (transfer to the FAT32 image and the effect of recalibration).] Exact duplicates between the official FFT-75 splits are few and change accuracy by only {{results/runs/cnn_m_s0/analysis/trust.json:duplicates/official_train/all_minus_without/accuracy|pct2}} points. Future work includes other file systems and cluster sizes, class-conditional conformal sets, larger real-media corpora and file-disjoint evaluation.
